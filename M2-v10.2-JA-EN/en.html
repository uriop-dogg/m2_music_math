Why are the notes of the major scale arranged this way?

The major scale is generally defined by the following values in twelve-tone equal temperament.

Major scale (12ET)

Hover over the floating ratios to hear them. The waveform and volume control are at the top right.

In practice, we rarely memorize these numbers. We are more likely to remember the layout of each key on an instrument as a pattern, or to work with intervals and the spacing of whole tones and semitones.

When I began studying music theory, I was surprised that the familiar do–re–mi–fa… (C–D–E–F… in the C-major example used here) was not an evenly spaced sequence of frequencies—or, more precisely, of logarithmic frequencies. On the piano, E and F are adjacent white keys, yet the interval between them is only a semitone.

I kept imagining that this familiar musical scale might have a simple underlying structure that science could describe.

Few people would regard its definition in 12ET as the origin of the major scale. Historically, mathematicians and musicians explored many tunings before a theory of music developed within 12ET, whose sound changes relatively little when transposed to different keys.

The major scale is commonly said to originate in simple integer ratios between frequencies. The major chord is a familiar example: the frequencies of C–E–G have the ratio

4:5:6=1:54:32

Following the reasons behind H-Major offers a way to understand why humans can acquire relative pitch, and what major tonality means in terms of sound. This site will explore those questions in detail.

01

What high C and low C have in common

Before discussing the auditory phenomena that humans perceive as music, we need an important premise: the pitch-class principle.

The pitch-class principle

Powers of two ( 2k , where k is any integer, including negative integers) may be disregarded in the perception of pitch class. In other words, when two frequencies differ by a factor of 2k , humans can recognize a shared identity in them. I call that identity their pitch class. □

This is what it means for both high C and low C to be C. “Disregarded” does not mean that we lose the ability to distinguish their pitches: we can recognize a shared identity despite their separation in pitch. This applies to arbitrary frequencies, not just those in 12ET, and the space of all perceivable pitch classes forms a circle. This circle corresponds to what music psychology calls “chroma.” For simplicity, and because this discussion extends somewhat beyond the usual scope of that term, I use “pitch class” here.

02

From harmonics to odd-numbered series

The next important auditory concept is harmonics. In Japanese these are usually called baion; here I use the English term “harmonics.” I define the following principle.

The harmonics principle

Humans can perceive a sequence of frequencies arranged as a harmonic series as a single group. □

Harmonics 1–16. Each tone continues sounding as the next is added.

When harmonics are played successively or simultaneously, we can recognize them as a series belonging to the same group. Put simply, we can recognize harmonics as harmonics. Combining this with the pitch-class principle reveals an important auditory theorem.

The odd-harmonics theorem

By the pitch-class principle, harmonics related by a power of two are recognized as the same pitch class. In this sense, only the odd harmonics provide independent pitch classes within a harmonic series. □

Odd harmonics: 1, 3, …, 15. Pitch classes: hold the root while following 1, 3, 5, 7… slowly, at 1.4-second intervals.

When referring specifically to this group as pitch classes, I call it an odd-harmonic pitch-class series, sometimes shortened to an odd-harmonic series or an odd series. This series will be central to our discussion of pitch-class relationships in harmony.

This alone may not persuade everyone. You might be thinking, “You defined the harmonics principle to make Harmonics Major work.” But I can explain why harmonics deserve this emphasis, and why hearing supports it. The key is combination tones.

04

Listening to two tones together

Combination tones have frequencies obtained by applying integer coefficients to sounding frequencies. For example, for two frequencies f1, f2(f1<f2 ), these include

f2 – f1, f1+f2, 2×f1 – f2…

Zero-frequency results are excluded. Human hearing is said to be able to perceive such frequencies. There are studies supporting this, and you can try listening for them here.

In my experience, combination tones are easier to grasp as a change in the quality of a sound than as separate tones. Listen to these examples. Can you hear a difference between playing the first and second tones separately and playing them together?

To me, saying that the two tones “interfere” captures the impression, and I think it is also close to the physical phenomenon. I interpret the trembling or roughness heard when they sound together—different listeners may describe it differently—as an integration of several combination-tone components presumed to arise in the ear, or perhaps in perception within the brain.

Since combination tones have frequencies, it may also be possible, with effort, to perceive them as pitches. This can in fact be proved mathematically for general combination tones.

Closure of an odd-harmonic pitch-class series under combination tones

The pitch classes of combination tones from any chord built from an odd-harmonic pitch-class series always return to that same series. □

Under the harmonics principle, humans can recognize harmonics as a group—as one series. Taking octave equivalence into account, it therefore poses no problem if a combination tone falls outside the pitch classes actually sounding in the chord: it still belongs to the root’s harmonic pitch-class series. The chord and its combination tones, considered together, still belong to the same harmonic series.

This is why I came to think that harmonics deserve special emphasis. Harmonics draw even their combination tones into their own series.

The next section gives a mathematical proof of this closure.

06

Proving closure under combination tones

I said “the same series” because an odd-harmonic series is defined relative to a reference frequency, fref . The harmonics of C and those of F, although they do share some tones, differ as a whole and can be regarded as different series. Among such series with different reference frequencies, the claim is that combination tones return to the harmonic series of the same reference.

For an odd-harmonic pitch-class series with reference frequency fref , an arbitrary member can be written as

fi=2ki×qi×fref

Here, ki is the exponent of the power of two that determines octave placement, and may be any integer, including a negative one. qi is odd, so fi can be regarded as an odd multiple of fref placed in an arbitrary octave.

The combination tones arising from a chord built from this odd-harmonic series can then be written as

fcomb=|∑imi×fi|

mi is an integer coefficient. The expression gives the combination-tone frequency fcomb obtained by assigning integer coefficients to frequencies from the odd-harmonic series. The absolute value is used because a negative frequency has no meaning here.

Since fref itself is positive, we have

fcomb=fref×|∑imi×2ki×qi|

Some values of ki may be negative, so the quantity inside the absolute value need not be an integer. Let ki have minimum value A = min ki among the terms under consideration. Then

fcomb=2A×fref×|∑imi2ki−A×qi|

The absolute-value expression is now a nonnegative integer. Call it P. Since we exclude zero-frequency combination tones, fcomb ≠ 0 implies P ≠ 0. Thus P is a positive integer and can be written as

P=q×2l

Here, q is odd and l is a nonnegative integer.

Consequently,

fcomb=2A+l×q×fref

In pitch-class terms,

fcomb∼q×fref

The combination tone therefore belongs to the original odd-harmonic pitch-class series.

For the odd-harmonic pitch-class series generated by the reference frequency fref , every combination tone arising within it belongs to the same series.

07

Coordinates for pitch classes

The preceding proof used frequencies directly for clarity, but the subject is pitch classes. The odd-harmonic pitch-class series based on fref and that based on 2k ×fref are identical. This suggests that the reference of an odd-harmonic pitch-class series should itself be considered as a pitch class. Let us examine that idea.

Let us call the frequencies discussed so far physical frequencies, denoted by f, a positive real number. A standard representation of pitch class is

h=log2(ff0)mod1

I call this a pitch-class coordinate. Taking a base-two logarithm turns an octave upward into +1 and an octave downward into −1. Disregarding integer differences (mod 1) extracts the pitch-class information alone. The values of h lie in [0, 1), with 0 and 1 identified, so the resulting space is a circle.

f0 determines the origin of the pitch-class coordinates. In practice, f will usually be a frequency in hertz from an audio spectrum, so setting f0 = 440 Hz, the standard A4 tuning frequency, is convenient. The origin is then A in 12ET at A4 = 440 Hz, and ordinary 12ET pitch-class positions are simply

h12ET=k12mod1

Here, k is an integer.

09

Conclusion & An introduction to minor tonality

How was it? We have considered “combination tones” arising in the human ear and the odd-harmonic series as a series consistent with them. I believe I have now shown that, within the “earliest part” of those odd harmonics—the odd harmonics of 1 and of 3—lies hidden a tuning remarkably similar to the major scale used in contemporary music.

I also argue that conventional just intonation is inconvenient in this respect: the reference of its encompassing odd-harmonic series is F, the fourth degree of the key. The note we want to perceive as the tonic is therefore displaced from the reference of the series in which the combination tones align.

If harmonics appear to be the origin of the major scale, what changes for music? Scientifically, I see a clue for studying human perception of tonality: why does a piece feel bright or dark, and how does that work? I imagine that research into the harmonic aspects of music could help build a better mathematical space for musical expression—perhaps a theory of all the “colors” of sound humans can perceive, analogous to color science.

Within musical culture, people have long sought ways to produce just intervals. I have heard that musicians playing acoustic instruments still practice and perform with these intervals in mind; knowledge of harmonics should be useful there. In practice, though, just intonation may often be understood as the possibility of more beautiful harmonies beyond equal temperament, while musicians rely on the refined ears and sensibilities of teachers and artists rather than strict numerical tuning. I do not reject that; I think it is a very healthy attitude. After all, I favor Harmonics Major over conventional just intonation. If H-Major were taken as “true,” the conventional just-intonation settings of digital pianos would themselves be problematic. Either way, it may be better to trust human harmonic hearing than to force every note onto a tuner. There is also the issue of modulation. My aim is not to impose rigid mathematical answers on music, but to explore what kind of mathematical map we can draw across the world of musical expression.

These findings could also be used directly in electronic music. Equal temperament can be understood as a tuning designed to preserve workable harmony through every transposition on instruments with fixed tuning, such as pianos. With electronic instruments or music-production software, suitable tools could let us move the tonic freely while tuning melodies and chords to just ratios according to context. Microtonality and equal temperaments with arbitrary numbers of divisions seem to be attracting interest, but fractional intervals can also be specified directly, without going through an equal-division system. I would welcome more composition that works directly with just intonation. Such experiments might reveal more about just tuning and harmonics, deepening the exploration of human musical sensibility.

I also suspect that much music composed in modern equal temperament is a projection of its composer's harmonic sensibility onto that tuning. Odd-harmonic intervals do not coincide with equal-tempered ones. Repeated pure fifths, as in Pythagorean tuning—repeated multiplication by 3 under octave equivalence—never close into an exact finite cycle. If equal temperament approximates the harmonic world while making it finite and closed under transposition, what kinds of movement become possible within a tuning that approximately reproduces tonal and harmonic feeling? That is how I think about composition in equal temperament.
