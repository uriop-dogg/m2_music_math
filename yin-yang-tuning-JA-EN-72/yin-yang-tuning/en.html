A review of odd harmonics

In my article two posts back (Exploring Major and Minor Tonality I), I discussed how, if we take account of octave equivalence in human hearing—the sense that notes an octave apart share a quality—only the odd harmonics are independent as types of notes, or pitch classes, when octave height is disregarded (the Odd Harmonics Theorem). This follows from a simple calculation: an octave doubles the frequency, so we have the following.
The “notes” in each vertical column are related by octaves, so they can be regarded as the same pitch class. But look at what happens when we follow the integers in order.
The harmonics are the integers 1, 2, 3, …, but they repeatedly revisit the same pitch classes. In that sense, the series is redundant as a list of pitch classes. That is why I decided to use odd harmonics as the basis for distinguishing pitch classes. You could even call odd numbers the elemental building blocks of pitch classes.
There is, however, a point I forgot to clarify in that earlier article—or perhaps I simply explained it poorly. It is easy to misunderstand, so I would like to add a clarification here.
When I say that these elemental building blocks, the odd numbers, are independent, I mean specifically within a world defined by one chosen fundamental frequency or reference pitch class.
In other words, the “1” of a harmonic series does not mean 1 Hz. For a fundamental frequency f0, the sequence f0, f0×2, f0×3, f0×4, f0×5, … is the harmonic series of f0.
This is a little difficult to explain. Perhaps we could call it a “harmonic context.” Think of it as something that is established when you choose one fundamental frequency. In the harmonic context of a fundamental frequency f0, the odd terms f0, f0×3, f0×5, f0×7, f0×9, … are independent as pitch classes. That is how I think about it.
If we want to identify distinct pitch classes across the entire range of frequencies, without depending on one fundamental, there is no need to select odd terms of a harmonic series. We can simply take octave equivalence classes, identifying frequencies that differ by a power of two. As introduced in that earlier article, mathematically this is
h=log2(ff0)mod 1
In other words, we take the base-two logarithm and disregard integer differences, retaining the fractional part. This gives us a circle.
Although pitch classes form a circle, we cannot ignore octave height in actual sound. Yet the pitch-class property is still there. A geometric model of musical pitch is therefore a helix. This model is also discussed in music psychology and related fields.

The subharmonic series

In music theory, this is called the subharmonic series. Whereas the harmonic series consists of integer multiples of a frequency,
the subharmonic series consists of reciprocal-integer multiples:
That is, it is a series formed by multiplying by the reciprocals of integers.
A criticism often directed at theories involving the subharmonic series is:
♟
“The subharmonic series does not exist, so the theory falls apart!”
But I think this probably misses the point. Even the reasons for considering the harmonic series in music theory need not be limited to the fact that instrumental sounds contain harmonics. The same applies to the subharmonic series. I regard harmonic and subharmonic series as concerning “properties of sound and relationships between frequencies that deserve attention because sound consists of waves”. Chord relationships in just intonation still hold for sine waves without harmonics. I suspect this is closely related to nonlinear vibration in the human ear: combination tones, difference tones, and “harmonics” from self-interaction can arise even with pure-tone input. But I will not go into that here.

“A minor chord is a major chord with its major third lowered a little.”
“A minor chord sounds sad because it is a disrupted form of the harmony of a major chord.”
I do not think these descriptions are necessarily wrong, but perhaps we could pay more attention to the fact that a minor chord can be derived from the subharmonic series. I imagine I am not the only person who favors that perspective.

04

A method for tuning all diatonic chords in just intonation

This discussion has grown quite long, so I would like to move directly to my answer for tuning all diatonic chords in just intonation.
One caveat first. The following claim:
“I have discovered a complete method, overlooked in conventional just intonation, for tuning all diatonic chords in just intonation using a single tuning.”
is not what I am claiming. I use two tunings. Or rather, essentially one, but with two versions. I call the original tuning the positive tuning, and the other tuning generated from it the negative tuning. Partly, I use these names because I like how they sound. The Japanese terms 陽 (yō) and 陰 (in) are intended in the senses of positive and negative. In a sense, the way this tuning is constructed does the same thing as the popular idea of “negative harmony.”

05

A recap and a digression

That is the method I found for tuning all diatonic chords in just intonation. As noted, it means that some degrees of the major scale can have two versions, depending on the context. However, because we multiplied by 5, the third degree of Harmonics Major, Mi, has only one version. Do and Mi—1 and 5—remain fixed.
I consider these two “fixed points” to be the “roots (tonal centers)” of this negative-and-positive tuning method. In this tuning, we can play various chords, primarily the diatonic ones. Considered as a whole, they necessarily belong to
Harmonic series of Do = 1
or
Subharmonic series of Mi = 5
—or to both.
So we might call Do the positive pole or positive root, and Mi the negative pole or negative root. But I am not insisting on those names; call them whatever you like. “Positive center” would work too.
Do–Mi (a major third—although naming an interval may not be entirely accurate when octave shifts are allowed) strikes me as a strong chord, with a sharp sense of cohesion to the ear, or to our sense of pitch. These notes remain fixed within this tuning, acting as centers of attraction around which the other notes gather to color our sense of tonality.
By the way, I have made an app that lets you perform using the negative-and-positive tuning introduced here!
More precisely, it is a modified version of the Harmonics Keyboard app I made earlier. The previous version already supported the negative-and-positive tuning described here, but I have reshaped it to make those features easier to use.
The app assigns the pitches of negative-and-positive tuning to a computer keyboard so that you can play them. By default, pentatonic scales are arranged in horizontal rows, but you can rearrange the layout to your liking. To play chords with multiple simultaneous keypresses, I recommend a gaming keyboard or another keyboard that can register the required keys independently.
P followed by a number denotes the positive tuning, numbered in ascending major-scale order. N followed by a number denotes the negative tuning, likewise numbered in ascending major-scale order. O1, however, is “1.” Since it is shared by both tunings, it uses a shared symbol.
You may be wondering, wasn't Mi shared too? Yes, it is when we use the negative tuning multiplied by 5. But the app extends the case discussed here a little, which brings me to a digression.
Here we multiplied the reciprocal series of the positive tuning by 5. This moves the reference to the third odd term of the harmonic series. What happens if we multiply by 3 or by 1 instead?
Multiplying by 1 gives a somewhat incomplete, but slightly minor-sounding form. (This is the simple reciprocal series of Harmonics Major shown above ↑) But multiplying by 3 is striking. I will skip a detailed explanation, but at exactly this multiplier, N1–N7 form a minor scale parallel to P1–P7, sharing the same tonic.
Recall the relationship between the subharmonic series and minor chords. In the sequence 1, 13, 15, it was 13 that served as the root. Here, too, multiplying by 3 makes the position 13 coincide with “1”—the “1” of the positive tuning, Do. The result is a scale built around the Do–Mi♭–Sol chord.

Now, Vm in this threefold negative tuning is not tuned as a just-intonation minor triad… Is that a fatal flaw? Perhaps not:
V is actually available on the positive side. So, when playing a cadence,
Ⅰm → Ⅳm → Ⅴ → Ⅰm
is natural in this tuning. That is harmonic minor.
Could this be why the dominant in a minor key becomes major—why Ti♭ is raised by a semitone?!! Sorry, I got carried away. The people who developed harmonic minor probably did not arrive at it through this reasoning, so I do not think that is the explanation in terms of tuning history.
Even so, I find this negative-and-positive tuning method fascinating: it is packed with interesting phenomena and ideas that could lead to “harmonic explanations” of music theory.
This time I explored relative keys, diatonic chords, and parallel keys. Expanding the idea may reveal even more interesting results. Please try the keyboard—or make your own app using negative-and-positive tuning—and play, explore, and research it for yourself.
